What Is Naam Jaap?

Naam jaap (नाम जप) is the repetition of a divine name or mantra, such as Ram, said quietly or aloud with attention. Devotees traditionally count the repetitions on a japa mala of 108 beads, moving one bead for each name. This jaap counter does the same work on your phone: one tap is one bead, and after 108 taps one mala is complete.

Why 108?

A japa mala has 108 beads and one guru bead, the sumeru, which marks the start and end of a round. The number 108 is held sacred in Hindu tradition, and one full round of the mala is counted as 108 repetitions. Counting in malas makes it easy to keep a daily sankalp, such as one, five or eleven malas.

Ram Naam Jaap

Ram Naam jaap is the most widely practised naam jaap among devotees of Shri Ram. In Shri Ramcharitmanas, Goswami Tulsidas describes the name of Ram as a jewel-lamp placed at the doorway of the tongue, bringing light within and without. Many devotees chant राम or the full taraka mantra, श्री राम जय राम जय जय राम, and count their malas each day. For guidance from Shri Ram on a question, see the Ram Shalaka Prashnavali.

How to Do Naam Jaap

There is no single rule for naam jaap, but the traditional method is simple and most devotees follow it closely.

  • Time and place. Early morning or evening is preferred, but any time is accepted. Sit in a clean place, on an asana, with the spine straight, facing east or north if you can.
  • The mala. A japa mala has 108 beads and a guru bead, the sumeru. Hold it in the right hand and move the beads with the thumb and the middle finger. The index finger is not used. When you reach the sumeru, do not cross it: turn the mala and continue.
  • The name. Say it clearly and at a steady pace, with the mind on the name and not on the count. One name is one bead.
  • Three ways. Vachik jaap is spoken aloud, upanshu is whispered so that only you can hear it, and manasik is silent, in the mind. Manasik jaap is held to be the highest, and spoken jaap the easiest to begin with. Likhit jaap, writing the name by hand, is a fourth practice.
  • The sankalp. Decide on a number of malas a day that you can keep, and keep it every day. A small sankalp kept is valued above a large one broken.

This counter stands in for the mala. Each tap is a bead, and a mala is counted when you reach 108.

What the Scriptures Say About the Name

Goswami Tulsidas gives the name of Ram a central place in Shri Ramcharitmanas. In Bal Kand he writes:

राम नाम मनिदीप धरु जीह देहरीं द्वार।
तुलसी भीतर बाहेरहुँ जौं चाहसि उजिआर॥

Place the jewel-lamp of the name of Ram at the threshold of the tongue, says Tulsidas, if you want light both within and without.

In Uttar Kand he goes further and names jaap as the practice for this age:

कलिजुग केवल नाम अधारा।
सुमिरि सुमिरि नर उतरहिं पारा॥

In the Kaliyug the name alone is the support. Remembering it again and again, people cross over. This is why devotees of Shri Ram keep a daily count of Ram naam, and why the Ram Shalaka Prashnavali is drawn from the same text.

Stories of the Name

Valmiki and the reversed name

According to tradition, the sage Valmiki began life as Ratnakar, a robber. The sage Narada told him to repeat the name of Ram, but he could not bring himself to say it. Narada asked him to repeat "mara" instead, the word for death. Said again and again without a pause, "mara mara mara" became "Rama Rama Rama". Ratnakar sat in jaap so long that an anthill, a valmik, grew over him, and he rose from it as Valmiki, who went on to compose the Ramayana. The story is told to show that the name works even when the one who says it does not yet understand it.

Hanuman and the floating stones

In a tale told across India, the vanaras building the bridge to Lanka wrote the name of Ram on each stone, and the stones floated. When Shri Ram himself threw a stone into the sea, it sank. Hanuman explained that what Ram lets go of sinks, and what carries his name is carried. Devotees of Hanuman Ji tell it to explain why he is called the greatest servant of the name.

The Ram Naam Bank of Prayagraj

In Prayagraj a bank has run for about a century with no money in it. Its depositors write "Ram" in booklets, 108 times to a page, and deposit the filled booklets in their own account. More than a hundred thousand people hold accounts, from several faiths, and booklets arrive from abroad. During the Kumbh Mela the bank opens a stall on the riverbank. It is likhit jaap on the scale of a city.

How many malas should I do in a day?

There is no fixed rule. Many devotees take a sankalp of a set number of malas each day, such as one, five or eleven, and keep it up without a break.

Which mala should I use?

Devotees of Shri Ram and Vishnu traditionally use a tulsi mala, and devotees of Shiva a rudraksha mala. Any mala of 108 beads serves, and this counter can take its place.

Does jaap count without a mala?

Yes. The mala only keeps the count. Many devotees count on the joints of the fingers, and silent jaap through the day is done with no count at all.

How fast should I chant?

Steadily, at a pace where each name is clear and the mind stays with it. Most people take a few minutes for one mala. Speed earns nothing.

Can I do jaap while walking or working?

Silent jaap can be done anywhere, and the tradition encourages it. Keep the counter for a seated session, when you can give the name your full attention.
