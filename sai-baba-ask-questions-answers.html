Sai Baba of Shirdi: Life and Storiesशिरडी साईं बाबा का जीवन और कथाएँ

Who Was Sai Baba?

Sai Baba of Shirdi was a saint who lived in the village of Shirdi, in what is now Maharashtra, for about sixty years, until his passing on 15 October 1918. No record says where he was born or what his name was. By tradition he was born in 1838 at Pathri, came to Shirdi as a youth, left, and returned around 1858 with a wedding party, this time for good.

He lived in a ruined mosque he named Dwarkamai, kept a sacred fire burning there, begged his food from a few houses each day, and gave away whatever he received. Hindus and Muslims both counted him as their own. He taught in short sayings and in what he did, and his devotees wrote down what they saw. Those accounts became the Shri Sai Satcharitra. Stories from Sai Baba's Life

These are the stories devotees tell most often, retold here in short form from the accounts in the Sai Satcharitra.

The lamps lit with water

Every evening Baba lit oil lamps in Dwarkamai, begging the oil from the village shopkeepers. One evening, the story goes, the shopkeepers agreed among themselves to refuse him, to see what he would do. Baba went back to the mosque without a word, poured water into the lamps, and lit them. They burned through the night. The shopkeepers came to ask his forgiveness, and he asked only that they never lie again. The story is told to show that he needed nothing from anyone, and that he held no grudge.

The dhuni and the udi

Baba kept a fire, the dhuni, burning in Dwarkamai day and night, and it burns there still. From its ash, the udi, he gave a pinch to everyone who came, to be swallowed with water or rubbed on the forehead. Devotees carried it home for the sick and the frightened. Udi is still given at Shirdi today, and the dhuni is the oldest living thing in the temple.

Shraddha and saburi

Asked what he wanted from those who came to him, Baba said he took two things from each: shraddha, faith, and saburi, patience. Have faith in me and wait, he said, and the rest will follow. These two words are painted all over Shirdi, and they are the shortest summary of his teaching.

The arm in the fire

One day in 1910 Baba sat by the dhuni and suddenly thrust his arm deep into the fire, holding it there until his attendant pulled him back. His arm was badly burned. Asked why, he said that a blacksmith's wife in a distant village, working at her furnace with her child on her hip, had let the child slip towards the fire, and he had put his arm in to catch it. The child was saved. The story is told to show that Baba's care did not stop at the walls of Shirdi.

The dakshina

Baba asked almost everyone who visited for dakshina, a gift of money, sometimes a rupee, sometimes a few. Visitors who did not understand thought him greedy. By evening every coin had been given away, to the poor, to the people who served him, to anyone in need, and he slept as he had woken, with nothing. Those who understood said that what he asked for was never the money. It was the giving.

The handi

On festival days Baba cooked for everyone himself, in great pots called handis, rice and dal and sweet dishes, with no distinction of caste or faith. The story remembered most is of him stirring the boiling pot with his bare arm, taking it out unharmed, and feeding the whole gathering. Food is still cooked and served to pilgrims in Shirdi every day.

Sabka Malik Ek

Baba would not say whether he was Hindu or Muslim. He lived in a mosque and called it Dwarkamai, after Krishna's city. He kept the fire, quoted from the Quran and the Hindu scriptures alike, and let the Ram Navami festival and the Urs be celebrated together on the same day. His constant saying was Sabka Malik Ek: everyone's master is one. The words are written above the door of Dwarkamai to this day.

The last days

In October 1918, after some days of fever, Baba told those with him that he was going and asked to be placed in the Wada, the building that is now the Samadhi Mandir. He passed on 15 October, Vijayadashami day, giving away his last few rupees to a devotee. Before he went he had said that his tomb would speak, and that he would answer anyone who called him with faith. That promise is why devotees still come to Shirdi, and why they ask him questions through the Sai Baba Prashnavali.

The Shri Sai Satcharitra

The Shri Sai Satcharitra is the account of Baba's life written by Govind Raghunath Dabholkar, a devotee whom Baba nicknamed Hemadpant. He asked Baba's permission to write it in 1916, wrote it in Marathi over the years that followed, and it was published in 1930, shortly after his death. It runs to about fifty chapters, mixing stories of Baba with his sayings and with Hemadpant's own reflections.

Devotees read it as a parayan, often over seven days, called a saptah, beginning on a Thursday, which is Baba's day. Authorised editions in many languages, including English and Hindi, are published by the Shree Saibaba Sansthan Trust, Shirdi, which also offers it to read on its website. Frequently Asked Questions

When was Sai Baba born?

By tradition on 28 September 1838 at Pathri, in the Parbhani district of Maharashtra. No record of his birth survives, and the date is a traditional one.

When did Sai Baba arrive in Shirdi permanently?

Around 1858, with the wedding party of Chand Patil of Dhoop village. He had visited earlier as a youth and left. This time he stayed for the rest of his life.

When did Sai Baba take Mahasamadhi?

On 15 October 1918, on Vijayadashami. His samadhi is in the Samadhi Mandir at Shirdi.

What are Sai Baba's best-known miracles?

Lighting lamps with water, putting his arm into the fire to save a child, stirring boiling food with his bare arm, curing the sick with udi, and appearing to devotees far from Shirdi.

Can I read the Sai Satcharitra online?

Yes. The Shree Saibaba Sansthan Trust, Shirdi, offers authorised editions, including English and Hindi, on its website.

How should I read the Sai Satcharitra?

Devotees read it as a parayan, often over seven days starting on a Thursday. There is no rule. Reading one chapter a day with attention is also a full reading.
