Today's Panchang

A panchang is the Hindu almanac: the day's tithi, nakshatra, yoga and karana, the weekday, and the times of the day that tradition keeps or avoids. All of it hangs on the local sunrise, so a devotee in New Jersey or Sydney needs a panchang for New Jersey or Sydney, not for Delhi.

How these times are worked out

  • The positions of the Sun and the Moon come from the JPL DE421 planetary ephemeris, the data astronomers use.
  • Sunrise and sunset are the moments the upper edge of the Sun crosses the horizon, allowing for the bending of light in the air, as most panchangs print them. Moonrise and moonset are for the centre of the Moon.
  • Nakshatra, yoga and the rashis use the Lahiri (Chitrapaksha) ayanamsa, the standard of India's national calendar. The tithi needs no ayanamsa: it is the angle between the Moon and the Sun.
  • Rahu Kaal, Yamaganda and Gulika are eighths of the day from sunrise to sunset. Abhijit is the eighth of the day's fifteen muhurtas, and Brahma Muhurta the second-last muhurta of the night.
  • Times are rounded to the nearest minute. Another panchang may differ by a minute or two where its conventions differ.

Frequently Asked Questions

Which tithi do I follow for a fast?

For most observances, the tithi in force at sunrise where you are. Some fasts turn on a particular moment instead, such as moonrise or midnight, and their rules differ. This page gives the times; the rule belongs to the tradition you follow.

What is Rahu Kaal?

One eighth of the daytime, a different eighth on each weekday, which tradition avoids for beginning new work. It is counted from the local sunrise, so it shifts with the season and the place.

What does "till" mean?

The time at which that tithi or nakshatra ends. From then until the next sunrise, the next one named is in force.

Is this a horoscope?

No. A panchang is a calendar: the positions of the Sun and the Moon and the divisions of the day. It predicts nothing.
